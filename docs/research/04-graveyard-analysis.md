# 04 — Graveyard analysis: dead computer-vision lifting and form-analysis apps
Ticket: [#4](https://github.com/jirigrill/snatch-exercise-validation/issues/4) · Status: resolved · Date: 2026-09-14

## Headline

The graveyard is real, it is large, and it is remarkably consistent: **almost nothing in camera-based movement analysis has ever died of an accuracy failure. It died of a demand failure.** Coach's Eye — the category-leading video-analysis app for coaches, 11 years old, with a working product and paying customers — was retired by a profitable parent company that "stepped away from sports video analysis entirely to focus on its other products." Amazon killed the most rigorously validated consumer movement-assessment product ever shipped (Halo Movement, peer-reviewed in *npj Digital Medicine*) citing "an increasingly crowded segment and an uncertain economic environment" after "piddling sales." Tempo raised roughly $300–398M to do 3D form tracking and abandoned the hardware; Peloton discontinued its camera. The single closest analogue to the product under test — Onyx, a YC-era phone-camera form-correction app with hundreds of thousands of users — never raised a seed round and was sold to an Indian gym chain for its technology. Form analysis is repeatedly a **feature that gets acquired, not a business that compounds.**

## Method and evidence quality

General-purpose WebSearch was unavailable for this run (backend validation errors on every call; Google, Bing, Brave, Mojeek, Ecosia and SearXNG instances all returned CAPTCHAs or junk). Search was performed instead through Google News RSS, the Hacker News Algolia API, direct fetches of vendor and archive pages, live DNS/HTTP status checks of company domains, and a text-extraction proxy over DuckDuckGo Lite. Reddit and the Internet Archive were both partially unreachable, so some founder post-mortems are cited via search-result snippets rather than the primary thread and are marked **SECONDARY** accordingly.

Two structural caveats. First, **funding figures for private companies come from data aggregators (Tracxn, PitchBook, Crunchbase snippets) and are marked SECONDARY** — they are directionally reliable, not audited. Second, **"cause of death" is often not publicly stated.** Where a company simply stopped, the honest entry is "Cause of death NO EVIDENCE FOUND," and that phrasing is used below rather than a plausible-sounding guess.

## Case detail

### Coach's Eye (TechSmith) — the most important case

This is the one that should worry the product under test most, because **nothing went wrong with the product.** Coach's Eye was the category-defining video-analysis app for coaches across every sport: record on any device, slow-motion scrub, telestration, side-by-side compare, athlete sharing. It was used at the Rio Olympics, integrated with GoPro, and sold at roughly $120/year for a coach. It had eleven years of continuous operation and a large installed base (50,000+ Android installs on the final delisted build alone).

TechSmith retired it anyway. Timeline **VERIFIED**: announcement email to customers on **14 September 2021**, a one-year fully-supported grace period, and complete shutdown on **15 September 2022** ([trackandfieldapp.com](https://www.trackandfieldapp.com/coachs-eye-alternatives-for-track-and-field/), [ippmusic.com](https://ippmusic.com/tag/coachs-eye-app/), corroborated by a customer blog dated 17 Sep 2021, [jasongaylord.com](https://www.jasongaylord.com/blog/2021/09/17/techsmith-coaches-eye-retiring)).

The retirement page itself gives **no reason at all** — a notable fact in its own right. Full text, **VERIFIED** by direct fetch of [go.coachseye.com/retirement](https://go.coachseye.com/retirement/) on 2026-09-14:

> "We are sorry to say that Coach's Eye has been retired and is no longer a working service. […] It has been an absolute honor to serve you over the past 11 years. […] It was a pleasure of a lifetime to go on this Coach's Eye journey with you."

No successor was released and TechSmith left the category entirely. The most specific published statement of cause is **SECONDARY**: TechSmith "did not release a successor to Coach's Eye and stepped away from sports video analysis entirely to focus on its other products" ([VisualEyes](https://www.visualeyesapp.com/coaches-eye-alternative), a competitor's comparison page — self-interested, but factually consistent with TechSmith's product line today, which is Camtasia and Snagit only). TechSmith's own support knowledge base no longer contains the retirement FAQ; the linked article ID 404s and search returns nothing on the topic. **The exact internal financial rationale is NO EVIDENCE FOUND.**

The inference that matters: a profitable, well-run software company with a working, category-leading, paid coaching-video product decided that product was not worth continuing to run. **INFERRED** — the revenue and margin were too small to justify the maintenance and platform-treadmill cost of a mobile video app, not that the product was bad.

### Amazon Halo / Halo Movement — accuracy was proven, and it did not matter

Halo Movement (launched Jun 2021) was smartphone-camera-based movement assessment: five exercises, computer vision, scores for mobility, stability and posture. Amazon published a **peer-reviewed validation** in *npj Digital Medicine* — intrasubject coefficient of variation 1.64% ± 1.42%, statistically significant correlation with all 13 clinical functional-movement reference tests (0.29 < r < 0.63), correlation with Xsens 3D mocap 0.23 < r < 0.83, and clean discrimination between athlete (86.6), healthy (81.3) and movement-impaired (68.4) cohorts. The authors concluded it "could be used in lieu of existing functional movement screens." **VERIFIED** ([npj Digital Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-022-00684-9), [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9445016/)). Caveat: Amazon-funded, three Amazon-employee authors, and the mobility/stability/posture sub-scores were explicitly *not* validated.

Amazon killed it anyway. Support ended **31 July 2023**; devices and app stopped working 1 August 2023; remaining health data deleted; 12 months of hardware purchases and unused prepaid subscriptions fully refunded. Amazon's public statement is pure non-explanation, **VERIFIED** ([About Amazon](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-halo-discontinued)):

> "At Amazon, we think big, experiment, and invest in new ideas like Amazon Halo in our efforts to delight customers. While we are proud of what we built, we recently made the difficult decision to stop supporting Amazon Halo effective July 31, 2023."

The internal reason is on the record via *The Verge*, which obtained the internal email — **VERIFIED**: Amazon was abandoning the category due to "significant headwinds, including an increasingly crowded segment and an uncertain economic environment," following "piddling sales." The same reporting confirms Amazon had a roadmap (codename "Donna") for a "Halo AI Trainer" that "would use computer vision to assess a user" — i.e. essentially the product under test, built by Amazon, cancelled before launch. A source noted: "The amount of data Amazon collected on [Halo] customers is incredible… But none of that data was enough to turn things around." ([The Verge, 1 May 2023](https://www.theverge.com/2023/5/1/23704825/amazon-halo-canceled-features-ai-training-apple-watch))

This case is the cleanest possible refutation of the accuracy hypothesis. Amazon had validated CV movement analysis, unlimited engineering budget, the largest distribution channel in retail, and behavioural cohort data on every user — and still could not make consumers pay for movement assessment.

### Onyx — the closest structural analogue to the product under test

Onyx is the case to study hardest, because it *is* the product under test with a different exercise selection: an iPhone app using on-device computer vision to count reps, correct form, and serve guided workouts, no hardware. Founded 2018 by Asaf Avidan Antonir and James Sha, YC-adjacent Silicon Valley, Forbes Under 30.

Outcome: acquired by India's Cure.fit, announced **January 2021**, price undisclosed. **VERIFIED** ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/curefit-acquires-digital-fitness-company-onyx-aims-to-accelerate-international-offering-301212500.html)). The acquisition rationale was explicitly technological: Cure.fit bought it "to improve its computer vision technology for its at-home fitness product and expand its international business."

The critical detail, **SECONDARY** via Forbes: "Onyx was looking for investors to raise a seed round, after it had raised a $1 million pre-seed in 2018. Instead, the company … got [an acquisition offer]." ([Forbes, 29 Jan 2021](https://www.forbes.com/sites/igorbosilkovski/2021/01/29/meet-the-under-30-entrepreneurs-who-just-sold-their-app-to-indian-fitness-company-curefit/)). A founder retrospective describes the company as built over ~2.5 years to ~10 people and "a few hundred thousand users" before selling. **SECONDARY** — the founder's own Reddit post-mortem exists ([r/EntrepreneurRideAlong](https://www.reddit.com/r/EntrepreneurRideAlong/comments/ya6voi/we_recently_sold_our_company_i_documented_our/)) but Reddit was unreachable from this environment, so the numbers are cited from search snippets and secondary summaries, not the primary text.

Read plainly: a technically competent CV form-check app reached hundreds of thousands of users, could not convert that into a fundable revenue story, and exited for its algorithms. **INFERRED**: the monetisation, not the vision model, was the binding constraint. Postscript — Cure.fit itself later collapsed as a growth story, so the Onyx codebase's second life was also short.

### Tempo and the connected-fitness form-tracking cohort

Tempo built the most expensive consumer form-tracking system ever shipped: a $2,500 6-foot cabinet with a 3D depth sensor giving real-time form correction on barbell and dumbbell lifts. Funding is reported at $220M Series C alone, with totals cited between ~$300M and $398M across aggregators (**SECONDARY**: [Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/tempo/__MprfUYMasaeKMrJrA3D7B_IcBGSl1q5FDwEEDAO6SVc)).

It did not die, but the hardware-and-form-tracking thesis did. In **August 2022** Tempo abandoned upfront hardware for a $59/month membership bundling the cheaper Move dock, "distancing itself from bulky hardware… The focus is coaching, not equipment," with the redesigned homepage making "virtually no mention of Studio." **VERIFIED** ([Fitt Insider, 4 Aug 2022](https://insider.fitt.co/tempo-pivots-to-new-membership-model/)). In **August 2023** it laid off close to half the company including coaching staff (**SECONDARY** — Reddit r/tempofitness, Glassdoor layoff reviews, and a laid-off product designer's public video; no press release found). tempo.fit still resolves and sells memberships as of 2026-09-14 (**VERIFIED** by HTTP check), with third-party ARR estimates around $15M (**SECONDARY**, GetLatka) — a fraction of capital raised.

The rest of the cohort is consistent. **Peloton Guide**, the $495 camera that counted reps and tracked strength form, stopped being sold on **30 July 2025**, described bluntly as a product that "never really had the name recognition" (**VERIFIED**, [Peloton Buddy](https://www.pelobuddy.com/guide-sale-ending/)). **lululemon** took a **$442.7M post-tax impairment** on Mirror in Q4 FY2022 and ceased selling the hardware and new digital subscriptions in **December 2023** (**VERIFIED**, [SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1397187/000139718726000020/R16.htm)). **Nautilus** bought CV company **VAY** in Sep 2021 and then filed Chapter 11 in March 2024, assets sold for $37.5M (**VERIFIED**). **Forme** discontinued its mirror and repositioned as a B2B Nasdaq micro-cap.

None of these failed because pose estimation was inaccurate. They failed on hardware unit economics and on demand that evaporated when gyms reopened.

### The sensor and wearable form-analysis graveyard

This cohort matters because these companies attacked the same job — "tell me if I'm lifting correctly" — with hardware, and were mostly wiped out. **Athos** embedded EMG sensors in athletic wear, raised roughly $35M, priced kits above $500, failed to convert consumers, pivoted to pro teams, stalled on custom-integration demands, and ran out of runway around 2020; liveathos.com no longer resolves (**VERIFIED** DNS failure 2026-09-14; cause **SECONDARY** via [unicornburn autopsy](https://unicornburn.com/autopsy/whoop-rival-athos-sports-wearable-usa)). **PUSH**, the leading consumer/coach velocity-based-training band, was acqui-hired by WHOOP in **September 2021** and the band is simply gone — "You can no longer train with PUSH or buy a PUSH band 2.0" (**VERIFIED**, [GymAware](https://gymaware.com/push-band-alternative/)); StrongFirst forum users were still hunting for a replacement in 2025. **Atlas Wearables** raised $500K+ on Indiegogo for "Exercise ID" rep recognition, was acqui-hired by Peloton, and Peloton has since said it will not build a wearable at all. **Moov** announced closure with a farewell note giving no reason. **Beast Technologies** is listed by Tracxn as no longer active. **Gymwatch, Notch Interfaces, Sensoria, Kinetic**: dormant or unverifiable, cause of death **NO EVIDENCE FOUND** for each.

The one B2B survivor pattern is instructive: **Perch**, camera-based velocity tracking sold to college and NBA weight rooms, was acquired by Catapult in June 2025 — i.e. it succeeded by selling to institutions, not to lifters.

### Golf and baseball: the mature analogue, and it did not produce a consumer subscription winner

Golf swing analysis is the strongest possible test of this business model: tens of millions of obsessive participants, decades of effort, and demonstrably high willingness to pay for instruction and equipment. If a consumer form-analysis subscription can work anywhere, it works here.

It has not produced an independent large-scale consumer subscription business. **Zepp Labs**, the swing-sensor and video-analysis leader, sold its core assets to Huami on **26 July 2018**; the Zepp brand is now a smartwatch company (**VERIFIED**, [Huami investor release](https://huami.investorroom.com/2018-07-26-Huami-Announces-Acquiring-Core-Assets-of-Zepp-International-Limited-and-Physical-Enterprises-Inc)). **Blast Motion**, with 500M+ logged swings, was acquired by WIN Reality in **June 2025** (**VERIFIED**, [SBJ](https://www.sportsbusinessjournal.com/Articles/2025/06/23/win-reality-acquires-blast-motion-for-integrated-vr-and-swing-sensor-technology/)). **Sportsbox AI** — genuinely impressive 3D-from-phone golf analysis, the technical state of the art, $15.99/month consumer subscription — was acquired in **April 2026** in an "eight-figure" deal led by Bryson DeChambeau after ~5 years and $5.5M+ raised (**VERIFIED**, [GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/golf-star-bryson-dechambeau-leads-acquisition-of-seattle-area-startup-sportsbox-ai/)). An eight-figure exit is a good outcome for founders; it is not evidence of a large durable subscription business.

Note also the golfer-side signal on trust: on GolfWRX, users comparing Sportsbox against Swing Coach AI report "Swing Coach thinks my swing is generally better than it is" — the credibility problem for AI form feedback among knowledgeable users is flattery, not merely error. **SECONDARY** ([GolfWRX](https://forums.golfwrx.com/topic/2061178-sportsbox-vs-swing-coach-ai-apps/)).

### The desktop survivors — what "success" looks like at this niche's true size

**Kinovea** is alive and actively developed (release 2025.1, November 2025), free, open-source, and funded by Ko-fi and Patreon donations to essentially one maintainer. **Dartfish** has existed since 1999, raised $14.6M, and is estimated at roughly $12.8M annual revenue selling to federations, universities and clinics (**SECONDARY**). Neither is dead. Both show the honest shape of the demand: video movement analysis sustains a donation-funded hobby project and a small institutional B2B vendor. It has not sustained a consumer subscription company.

### Olympic-weightlifting-specific apps

No Olympic-weightlifting-specific form-analysis app was found to have launched at scale and then vanished with a documented shutdown. **NO EVIDENCE FOUND.** This is not reassuring — issue #3 established that the extant weightlifting-analysis apps are tiny (WL Analysis: 100K+ Android installs at a one-time $8.99; the closest AI clone has too few ratings for Apple to display an average). Products that small die silently by delisting, leaving no announcement to find. **INFERRED**: the absence of a documented weightlifting graveyard reflects the absence of anything large enough to make news, not the absence of failure.

| Hardware unit economics / demand collapse | Tempo, Mirror, Forme, Athos, Nautilus/BowFlex, Beast | 6 |
| Founder execution / financial discipline | Gymtrack | 1 |
| **Accuracy failure** | **none found** | **0** |
| Cause of death NO EVIDENCE FOUND | Moov, Vi Trainer, Gymwatch, Notch, Sensoria, Kinetic | 6 |

Three things follow.

**1. Accuracy is not the failure mode, and building better accuracy does not address the actual risk.** Not one documented death in this category is attributable to the vision model being wrong. The most rigorously validated product in the set (Halo Movement, peer-reviewed, CV 1.64%) was killed by its owner for commercial reasons. If the product under test is betting that "our fault detection is finally good enough," it is optimising the one variable the graveyard says never mattered. **INFERRED** from the distribution above.

**2. Form analysis is repeatedly valued as a *component*, not as a *company*.** Ten of the documented outcomes are acquisitions in which the CV technology was absorbed by someone with an existing customer relationship — a gym chain (Cure.fit/Onyx), a wearable subscription (WHOOP/PUSH), a hardware maker (Nautilus/VAY, Peloton/Atlas), a VR trainer (WIN Reality/Blast), a team-analytics vendor (Catapult/Perch), a PT software vendor (Kinaxa/asensei). The consistent market verdict is that movement analysis is a feature that increases the value of an existing subscription, and is not itself a reason to start one. **INFERRED**, but this is the single most robust pattern in the data.

**3. Retention vs. acquisition — the evidence points at acquisition and monetisation, and *not* at the episodic-use hypothesis.** The brief anticipated a retention failure ("users check form once then stop"). **Direct evidence for that specific mechanism is NO EVIDENCE FOUND** — no company in this graveyard published cohort retention data, and Amazon, which had it, said only that the data "was not enough to turn things around." What *is* documented is upstream of retention: Amazon's "piddling sales" and "increasingly crowded segment," Peloton Guide's absent name recognition, Athos's price-blocked adoption, Onyx's inability to raise on a few hundred thousand users. Episodic use remains a plausible **ASSUMPTION** consistent with everything observed — Coach's Eye's own pricing model (a coach's seasonal tool) and Kinovea's donation economics both fit it — but the graveyard's proven killer is that not enough people ever cared enough to pay in the first place.

### Does the product under test avoid this pattern or repeat it?

It repeats the core pattern and avoids only the secondary one.

Avoided: **hardware.** A software-only phone app sidesteps the unit-economics trap that killed Tempo, Mirror, Forme, Athos, Beast and Bowflex. This is a real and significant advantage — six of the deaths above are simply not available to it.

Repeated, unmitigated:
- **Same value proposition Amazon abandoned after building it better.** Amazon's cancelled "Donna"/Halo AI Trainer was CV form assessment plus AI coaching plus adaptive programming. That is the product under test, cancelled pre-launch by the best-resourced possible builder.
- **Same category a profitable software company chose to exit.** Coach's Eye proved that paid coaching-video analysis can work as a product and still not be worth running as a business.
- **Narrower audience than any of the failures.** Onyx targeted all home fitness and reached only a few hundred thousand users. Sportsbox targeted golf, a vastly larger and richer market, and exited at eight figures. The snatch is a niche within a niche within a niche.
- **No existing subscription to attach to.** Every successful outcome in this graveyard was an attachment to someone else's customer relationship. A standalone snatch-analysis subscription is precisely the structure that has never worked.

One genuine differentiator deserves fair statement: **the AI narrative-coaching and corrective-programming layer is newer than most of this graveyard.** Every dead product here predates cheap high-quality LLM coaching, and issue #3 established that the money in lifting apps sits in *programming* subscriptions (JuggernautAI at $34.99/mo with 5,654 ratings), not in analysis. **INFERRED**: if this product survives, it survives as a programming subscription that uses form analysis as its acquisition hook and differentiator — not as a form-analysis product. That is a different business than the one described in the brief.

## Verdict impact

**Supports NO-GO as described. Does not kill the concept outright — but it does kill the concept as framed.**

This is a blocker ticket, so the explicit answer: **the graveyard does not independently kill the product.** The reason is a technicality that matters. The recurring, well-evidenced killers here are hardware unit economics and strategic abandonment inside larger companies. A one-person software product with a ~$1–3k/month net target is not exposed to those in the same way — it can survive at a scale that was a rounding error to Amazon, lululemon or TechSmith, and Kinovea and Dartfish prove that something persists at the bottom of this niche.

But the graveyard is strongly negative on the specific framing in the brief, on three counts.

First, **there is no evidence anywhere in this category that consumers sustain a subscription for form analysis.** Every documented attempt either exited as a feature, was abandoned, or persisted only via donations or institutional B2B sales. The bar of $1–3k/month net is modest, which is the concept's best defence; but issue #3's revealed price ceiling of a one-time $8.99 for barbell video analysis means clearing even that bar requires roughly 100–300 paying monthly subscribers in a niche whose most-adopted product could not persuade users to pay monthly at all.

Second, **accuracy investment is misallocated risk-reduction.** Zero deaths trace to accuracy; the technical work has no failure precedent to defend against, while the commercial failure mode has near-total precedent and no mitigation in the current plan.

Third, and most decisive, **the closest analogue by structure (Onyx) and the closest analogue by ambition (Amazon's cancelled Halo AI Trainer) both ended without ever finding a consumer willing to pay.** Onyx had the product working, hundreds of thousands of users, and still could not raise a seed. Amazon had validation, distribution and data and cancelled pre-launch.

Recommended reframe if the project proceeds: treat form analysis as the wedge, not the product, and make AI corrective programming the paid subscription — the one adjacent business in this space with demonstrated willingness to pay at $30+/month. Proceeding as "an AI snatch form-analysis subscription" is proceeding into the single most consistently unmonetisable product category surveyed here.
